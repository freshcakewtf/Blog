### Tiny Experiments → Creative Technologist

At the start of 2025, I set some strict goals.

Ship a game. Build new pipelines. Grow YouTube. Build a home lab. Rebuild my website. Turn experiments into opportunities.

I didn’t hit all of them.

But between the unfinished game, the hacked-together website, and a fart joke crossing a million views on YouTube, something more important happened:

I stopped treating my projects as random side quests  
and started treating them as a **career path**.

This was the year I funneled years of tiny experiments into one clear identity:  
**Creative Technologist.**  
And it led me from Nickelodeon to Adobe.

---

## Owning My Pipeline (and My Website)

For years, I paid for a Squarespace site loaded with features I never used.

It looked nice.  
It cost money.  
It didn’t really *do* anything.

This year, I admitted the obvious: my site had no focus and no real purpose. So I scrapped it.

I rebuilt everything using **GitHub Pages**, raw **HTML/CSS**, and a bit of ChatGPT support. No subscription. No templates. Just a low-cost publishing node I actually control.

The goal wasn’t “perfect portfolio.”  
The goal was: **creative sovereignty** — a system I can grow into, not out of.

It’s still evolving, but it’s mine.  
And the money saved? Redirected to the true subscription overlords: Disney+ and Hulu. Priorities.

---

## From Doomscrolling to Federation

Instagram became ads and doomscrolling, so I removed it from my phone.

That opened up space to ask: **Where do I share my work now?**

I landed on **Bluesky**, which pulled me deep into **AT Protocol**, federation, and the idea of owning your own data. I spent months learning the tech, experimenting, and even hacking pieces of it into my website.

It was quiet there. Hardly anyone saw my posts.  
But it aligned with my values, so I stayed.

That quiet ended up being good for me.

---

## The Fart Video and the YouTube Shift

While Bluesky was silent, something unexpected happened on YouTube:

A silly fart-joke video hit **1 million views**.

Not exactly the legacy I imagined, but it woke me up. YouTube wasn’t dead. The algorithm had opened the door again.

For years, I’d been “the Giphy guy” with billions of GIF views. Suddenly, YouTube was offering a new lane. So I started thinking:

> What if I didn’t just post animations…  
> but also shared **how** I make them?

That thought became the seed of a more intentional YouTube presence.

---

## Tiny Experiments, Not Big Pressure

The real turning point was a **book**:  
*Tiny Experiments* by Anne-Laure Le Cunff.

It reframed how I approached goals. Instead of giant, overwhelming objectives, everything became **small, testable experiments**.

I built a **scrum board** for my creative life—first in Apple Reminders, then eventually in **Obsidian** for more digital sovereignty.

Tasks became things like:

- “Make a YouTube Short about vector brushes in Fresco — due in 2 weeks”
- “Code a new About page with embedded YouTube video — due in 1 month”

Suddenly the pressure disappeared.

My rule:  
**If I run an experiment, I share the result.**

That became my content loop:

**Learn → Build → Post → Document → Share the documentation.**

Everything became multipurpose.

---

## Projects I Didn’t Ship (And Why That’s Fine)

Some goals didn’t make it across the finish line:

- ❌ *Never Done Jan* — Steam game  
- ❌ Fortnite island / PC build project  
- ❌ GIF licensing and passive platform push  

Old me would’ve been frustrated.  
New me sees them as:

- R&D testbeds  
- Tools for learning  
- Signals about where I actually want to go  

They taught me something more important than hitting a release date:  
I don’t want to just “publish a game.”  
I want a **sustainable creative pipeline** where art, code, and play all feed each other.

---

## Leaving Nickelodeon, Joining Adobe

This year, a bigger narrative snapped into focus.

The weird space I’ve always lived in—between art and code, animation and tooling, creativity and systems—finally got a name:

**Creative Technologist.**

And this identity wasn’t just a vibe.  
It became a job description.

2025 is the year:

- I **left Nickelodeon**, and  
- I **joined Adobe**.

All the tiny experiments, workflows, automations, and odd skills I’d accumulated suddenly made sense. They weren’t random—they were the foundation for the next chapter of my career.

---

## The Rise of the Creative Technologist (What’s Next)

If I had to summarize 2025:

> This was the year I stopped trying to do everything  
> and started building a system that lets me do the *right* things.

Going into 2026, I’m focusing on:

- **Creative sovereignty**  
- **Human-made, AI-assisted workflows**  
- **One centralized creative feed**, published everywhere  
- **Tiny experiments with long-term arcs**  

I didn’t ship every project.  
But I built the system, the identity, and the direction that make all future projects more meaningful.

Honestly, that feels like the best way to end 2025.
